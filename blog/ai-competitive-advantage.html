Does AI Give You a Competitive Advantage? Yes, If You Use It Well

AI can give a growing business hours back every week and the speed of a much bigger team. Where it pays off, the one trap to avoid, and how to keep your edge.

Arboreal Labs team27 October 20265 min read
Quick answer

Yes. AI gives a business a real competitive advantage when it takes over routine work like data entry, reports, follow-ups and first drafts, so a small team moves like a big one. The advantage lasts when AI is built around your own process and data, and your people keep the voice and judgement customers choose you for.

Does AI give your business a competitive advantage? For most growing businesses the honest answer is yes, and a bigger one than they expect. The question that matters more is how to use it so the advantage is yours, not the same one every competitor gets.

This topic came from Mehak Gupta, founder of Hereafter, a creative studio we work closely with on projects. We often debate the pros and cons of AI, and Aaron Horwath's essay Now Entering: The Era Of Bleh makes the strongest case for caution we have read.

Does AI give your business a competitive advantage?

Yes. Used on the right work, AI lets a small team do what used to need a much bigger one, faster and with fewer mistakes.

The gains are practical, not futuristic. Enquiries get a reply in minutes instead of the next morning. Reports that took an afternoon are ready when you open your laptop. Orders are entered once instead of four times. Each of those is time your team gets back for customers, sales and the work only they can do.

The advantage comes from how you use AI, not just whether. Two businesses with the same tools can get very different results.

What can AI do for a small business today?

More than most owners realise, and none of it needs a research team. The most reliable wins are in the work your team repeats every day:

  • Answering enquiries quickly: sorting new messages, drafting replies and flagging the urgent ones for a person
  • Ending double entry: capturing an order once and updating stock, invoices and dispatch automatically
  • Reports in seconds: weekly numbers pulled together and summarised, with anything unusual highlighted
  • Catching mistakes early: checking prices, quantities and dates before an error reaches a customer
  • Chasing for you: reminders for overdue invoices, approvals and follow-ups that used to rely on memory
  • First drafts: proposals, internal notes and documents your team then shapes and approves

None of these replace your people. They remove the parts of the job your people like least, which is usually where the most hours go. If spreadsheets are where most of that time disappears, these are the signs you have outgrown them.

What happens to businesses that wait?

They fall behind quietly. When a competitor replies to enquiries in five minutes and you reply the next day, the customer rarely waits to compare.

AI is quickly becoming standard equipment, much like email and websites before it. The businesses that start now learn what works for them while it is still an advantage. Those that wait end up adopting it anyway, just later and under pressure.

What is the catch with using AI for everything?

There is one real trap: handing all your thinking to the same tools as everyone else. The essay calls the result "airbrain", work that starts to look the same once every company runs it through the same AI.

You can already see it in websites, sales emails and LinkedIn posts that share the same tone and phrases. The essay compares it to a river in Florida that engineers straightened to stop it flooding; the bends had been doing jobs nobody counted, and putting them back cost over a billion dollars. Work has bends too, like the debate in a meeting or the second draft, and they are where your best ideas take shape.

So the risk is not AI. It is using it on the parts of your business that are meant to sound and think like you.

How do you get the most from AI without losing your edge?

Point AI at the routine, build it around your own way of working, and keep people on the decisions.

  1. Map your work. List your main processes and mark each one as routine or as something customers choose you for.
  2. Automate the routine first. That is where AI saves the most hours with no risk to what makes you different.
  3. Feed it your own knowledge. AI working from your data, your products and your past projects gives answers a competitor's tools cannot.
  4. Keep your voice on customer-facing work. Let AI do the first pass, and have a person make it sound like you.
  5. Keep people on the big calls. Pricing, strategy and anything with real money at stake gets a human decision.

Step 3 is where custom software earns its keep. An inventory management system built around one business's process, for example, automates the checks and updates nobody wants to do by hand, and leaves the decisions with the people who know the business.

Sprout’s tip

Ask your team which task they would happily never do again, then time it for one week. That number is usually the best case for your first AI project.

Where we stand in the AI debate

We use AI every day to build software faster, and we build it into the systems we make for clients. We are firmly on the side of using it.

Where we agree with the essay is that the advantage lies in where you use it. Generic AI on generic prompts makes businesses sound alike. AI built into your own process and working from your own data does the opposite, because no competitor has your process or your data. It is the same reason a custom website stands out where a template blends in.

The bottom line

AI is one of the best tools a growing business has ever had. Use it freely on the routine, build it around your own way of working, and keep your voice and judgement for the work customers see. Do that, and the advantage is yours rather than everyone's.

Frequently asked questions

Should a small business use AI?

Yes. Using AI for admin, data entry, reports and first drafts frees up hours every week and helps a small team respond as fast as a large one.

What should a small business automate with AI first?

Start with the jobs your team repeats every day and nobody enjoys: retyping orders, chasing invoices, sorting enquiries and pulling together weekly reports. They save the most hours and carry no risk to what makes you different.

Will AI make every business sound the same?

Only businesses that hand all their writing and thinking to the same AI tools without changing the output. Businesses that use AI for routine work and keep a human voice on customer-facing work will stand out more, not less.

Does AI-written content hurt my Google rankings?

Not just because AI helped write it. Search engines reward content that is genuinely helpful and original, so generic text that says what every other page says struggles to rank, whoever wrote it.
